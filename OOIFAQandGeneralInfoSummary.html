OOIFB- DSC Summary (What are the roles of the OOIFB and DSC? How do these groups inform NSF OOI practices?)#

The U.S. National Science Foundation (NSF) Ocean Observatories Initiative Facility Board (OOIFB) provides community-driven input and guidance regarding the management and operation of the NSF Ocean Observatories Initiative (NSF OOI). The OOIFB also works to expand scientific and public awareness of NSF OOI, and ensure that the oceanographic community is kept informed of developments of NSF OOI. The OOIFB established the Data Systems Committee (DSC) to help ensure timely and reliable access to high-quality OOI data. The DSC evaluates and recommends improvements to the data services policies and practices of the NSF OOI Facility that will lead to more efficient and effective scientific use of OOI data.

NSF OOI Summary (OOI infrastructure and systems, oceanographic practices)#

The Ocean Observatories Initiative (OOI) is made up of five major research components in the North and South Atlantic and Pacific (map of the NSF OOI arrays); two southern hemisphere arrays were decommissioned, although data are still available. A timeline of the overall OOI system is provided on page 11 of the Science Plan. Each array is composed of fixed and mobile platforms. Array locations and configurations were designed based on input from the scientific community to focus data collection aimed at specific regional and global science questions.

Please see below for a high level overview of each array including: regional oceanography, why is it located there, what makes it unique, research themes, and array components.

  1. The Regional Cabled Array (RCA) and its two sub-arrays – Cabled Axial Seamount and Cabled Continental Margin – on the Juan de Fuca plate

    1. Operated by the University of Washington (UW) (https://interactiveoceans.washington.edu/about/regional-cabled-array/ )

    2. Fiber optic cable(s) link instruments to shore, providing both power and two-way data transfer in real-time. Initial cable and instrument installation in 2014.

    3. Cabled Axial Seamount - Axial Seamount, Axial Base, and Mid-Plate nodes

      1. Axial Seamount (~1500 m depth) - science focus on volcanic and hydrothermal activity through seismology, geodesy, vent temperature monitoring, chemistry and video; wide range of benthic instruments at vents on both western and eastern sides of the caldera with geodetic instruments also in center of caldera; hydrography complex within caldera.

      2. Axial Base (deepest RCA site ~2600m) - science focuses on large-scale ocean behavior with large-scale currents (North Pacific/California Currents and the subpolar gyre) interacting in this region; main instruments are on deep and shallow profilers.

      3. The Mid-Plate node labeled on maps of RCA just has a primary node at this point. Primary nodes serve to transfer power and communications along the main cable and distribute it out to secondary nodes and extension cables. There are no instruments at the Mid_Plate node.

    4. Cabled Continental Margin - Slope Base and Hydrate Ridge plus connects with the Coastal Endurance Array.

      1. Slope Base - science focuses on ocean circulation and surface processes. Instruments at the seafloor are paired with shallow and deep profiles.

      2. Hydrate Ridge - science focuses on hydrate processes with instruments to detect bubble plumes and seismic activity.

  2. The Coastal Endurance Array off the coast of Oregon and Washington state.

    1. Operated by Oregon State University (surface moorings, profilers, and gliders) and the University of Washington (cabled seabed components).

    2. The Northern California Current is an eastern boundary upwelling system. Seasonal upwelling in the Northern California Current delivers cold, nutrient-rich deep water to the coast during the summer, fueling productivity and supporting fisheries, but upwelled water also contributes to episodic ocean acidification and hypoxia along the coast, which is further intensified by respiration on the shelf. This region experiences along-shelf variation in coastal circulation and biogeochemistry due to shelf topography and the influence of the Columbia River plume. This can lead to differences in stratification and upper ocean properties north and south of the plume.

    3. Coastal Endurance measures shelf oceanographic processes through:

      1. 2 sets of moorings to the North (WA) and South (OR) of the Columbia River plume.

        1. Moorings are located at the inner and mid-shelf, as well as on the seaward edge of the continental shelf.

      2. Gliders transect the shelf and supplement mooring data.

    4. Connection to the Regional Cabled Array

      1. The Cabled Continental Margin connects with the Coastal Endurance Array at the Oregon mid-shelf and offshore sites.

  3. The Coastal Pioneer Array is a moveable array that was originally deployed off the northeast U.S. coast and is currently deployed off the coast of northern North Carolina

    1. Operated by Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution.

    2. Coastal Pioneer Array New England Shelf (NES; 2013-2022; data is still available). The Coastal Pioneer NES Array was a seven-site mooring array located south of Martha’s Vineyard, Massachusetts. Mooring data were supplemented by six coastal gliders, two profiling gliders, and two autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs).

      1. The Continental Shelf-Slope area in this region is highly productive. It serves as a dynamic intersection where ocean currents meet in weather-like “fronts,” and where nutrients, pollutants, and other properties are exchanged between the coast and the deep ocean. Data from the inshore shelf area helped to examine exchanges between the shelf and slope and the shelf ecosystem, and provided broader insight into air-sea gas exchange, including carbon dioxide absorption.

    3. Coastal Pioneer Array Southern Mid-Atlantic Bight (relocated/installed, Spring 2024). This mid-Atlantic Bight (MAB) Array is located off the coast of Nags Head, North Carolina, in a region of complex confluence of several different water masses including Mid-Atlantic and South Atlantic Bight Shelf water, Virginia Coastal Water, Gulf Stream, Deep Western Boundary Current, and the Slope Sea.

      1. This confluence results in pronounced density gradients and fronts with differing biogeochemical properties over short spatial scales.

      2. The confluence results in significant cross-isobath transport that promotes shelf/ocean biogeochemical exchange.

  4. The Global Irminger Sea Array off the coast of Greenland

    1. Operated by Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution.

    2. Provides data at high latitudes that have not had extensive sampling.

    3. Area has high wind and large surface waves, making for strong atmosphere-ocean exchanges.

    4. Area of strong carbon cycling and deepwater thermohaline circulation.

    5. 2800m depth.

  5. Global Station Papa in the Gulf of Alaska

    1. Operated by Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution.

    2. Provides data at high latitudes, continuing observations at a site with a long observational history and relatively low advection and eddy energy.

    3. Additional assets at the site, including NOAA Pacific Marine Environmental Laboratory Surface Buoy and University of Washington waverider mooring.

    4. The area is vulnerable to ocean acidification and has a productive fishery.

    5. Impacted by Pacific Decadal Oscillation.

    6. 4200m depth.

  6. There are two additional Global Arrays that are no longer operational:

    1. Argentine Basin (data still available)

    2. Southern Ocean (data still available)

NSF OOI Array Platform Details and Instrument Tables#

General details about the diversity of instruments available through the platforms at each array are available online (and linked below). In addition to general instrument types, additional information on models and other instrument metrics can be found on other OOI webpages.

There are two general groups of instruments found on the OOI infrastructure: OOI-owned instruments and PI-owned instruments. The main OOI Instrument webpage lists all the general types for both categories, with links for more information.

  • OOI instruments are owned and operated by the OOI, were funded for OOI by NSF, and produce data for ingestion into the OOI data systems. The OOI Instrument webpage lists all the types of instruments operated by OOI. In some cases, there is more than one version (class and make) of an instrument (such as CTDs). The instrument pages list the data products, algorithms, and instances for each instrument type.

  • PI instruments are owned by a Primary Investigator (PI) directly. NSF encourages scientists to add new instruments or platforms onto the OOI facility. Funding for such additions typically comes from outside of OOI, e.g. a research proposal to NSF core programs or another agency. The OOI webpage on adding instruments or platforms addresses the steps required by PIs to propose the addition of new instruments, platforms, or infrastructure to the OOI facility.

Is there anything different about PI instrument data handling?

  • PI instruments are instruments added to OOI by individual researchers, but the data is made available to all.

    • https://oceanobservatories.org/pi-added-instruments-2/

    • https://oceanobservatories.org/how-to-participate/adding-instruments-or-platforms/

  • While most of the current PI instruments are on the Cabled Array, some are deployed on Endurance and Pioneer Array moorings. One was deployed on the Southern Buoy when that was operational. NSF welcomes applications to attach PI instruments to all the OOI infrastructure, whether funded by NSF or other sources.

  • For PI instruments on the Cabled Array the data are streamed or pulled in the same way as OOI Cabled Array data. They are available at http://piweb.ooirsn.uw.edu/. Data can be downloaded manually or using command line tools such as curl and wget..

  • For the current PI instruments on the Endurance Array, the data can be downloaded directly from the PI instrument webpage (which also describes something of how that data gets there).

  • PI data is generally served raw although some teams are also providing PI-processed data in the associated directory.

Regional Cabled Array (RCA) Instruments
Many of the instruments that comprise the RCA are located along the seafloor and generally focus on seafloor bottom pressure, fluids, or seismicity. The links below connect to tables listing the OOI instruments found at each site and node of the RCA.

  1. Cabled Axial Seamount Array Platforms and Instruments - These instruments are mainly tracking volcanic behavior, measuring seismicity, caldera floor elevation and tilt, or fluid discharge properties on the seafloor, although the Axial Base site includes both a shallow and a deep profiler looking at the water column.

  2. Cabled Continental Margin Array Platforms and Instruments - These instruments track hydrate discharge as well as plate tectonics and ocean properties. This site includes a shallow and a deep profiler looking at the water column.

  3. Cabled Endurance Array Platforms and Instruments - These instruments focus on the water column, including a shallow and a deep profiler.

Coastal Endurance Array Platforms and Instruments
The Coastal Endurance Array is made up of two cross-shelf moored array lines. Gliders move and collect data around, along, and between these lines. The moveable and non-cabled assets complement the cabled assets. The main focus is on properties of the water column. The linked sheet summarizes the instruments available.

Coastal Pioneer Mid-Atlantic Bight (MAB) Array
A combination of moorings and gliders focus on measuring properties of the water column. The linked webpage gives a tentative summary of the instruments available.

The two, active Global Arrays are both composed of a triangular set of moorings, with gliders also deployed in and around the arrays. Many of the instruments are deployed in the water column, measuring various physical, chemical, and biochemical properties.

Understanding the back-end of OOI data systems:

What are the different data levels for OOI data?

Where is data stored? What measures have been taken to ensure integrity of data into the future? How is data kept secure?

Hardware Summary:
The OOI Data Center is located at Oregon State University, in Corvallis, OR. The Data Center consists of an 8 PB Dell PowerScale storage cluster, a 24-node Apache Cassandra database, a 20-node JupyterHub cluster, a 16-node VxRail compute cluster, a Dell PowerProtect Data Domain, and a Dell Cyber Recovery Vault (see figure below). All this infrastructure is protected by a pair of Palo Alto firewalls. . Data are backed up daily and quarterly to geographically remote locations in Bend, OR and Austin, TX, respectively.

A graphical representation of OOI Data Center infrastructure and data flows. Systems in the orange box are located in Corvallis, OR

How does data flow from the instruments at sea into the Data Portal and other data access points?

Example data flow: Raw data files from the MIOs are stored on the PowerScale cluster. Servers running on the VxRail compute cluster parse the raw data files, and store the raw data in the Cassandra database and make them available, those data for M2M requests and data pulls that we do when we do sort of pre-compute files but yes, everything the primary, the initial landing, is on that Power Scale cluster.

  • OOI-CI maintains over 300 servers. Virtual machines are backed up daily to a Dell PowerProtect Data Domain and a Dell Cyber Recovery Vault located in Corvallis as well as the Elastic Cloud Storage cluster in Bend, OR.

  • Raw data are stored on the PowerScale cluster.

  • Raw data are backed up daily to an Elastic Cloud Storage cluster in Bend, OR, which provides an immutable, geographically remote backup of OOI data.

Broad Overview of OOI Data Flow (Software Highlights): Data comes into Ingest Engine, and then into Cupid, a queue for the data. In the queue there is AWIPS (a software package monitoring the queue), which holds EDEX. EDEX writes the data to the databases (e.g. Cassandra and Postgres), and also assists with data retrieval. Cassandra and Postgres work together as data comes in. Data can come in and be held in a queue if the system is offline for any reason.

Data can be accessed through M2M and EDEX (AWIPS). Stream Engine receives a request from OOINet, it calls EDEX, which brings the data back. This file is a NetCDF made up of many raw data files. OOINet still houses the data, but Data Explorer has replaced OOINet as an easier interface to examine the data. However, you cannot download raw data from Data Explorer currently (but links to the full resolution data are available and links to the raw data may be coming soon). It is faster because it uses Kafka to pull in pre-plotted data. Data Explorer was developed and is maintained by AXIOM. Two other ways to get data include the raw data server and JupyterHub.

OOI Data Flow:

  • Instruments (over 900 in the water), operating 24-7, push data to a land station. Data can be acquired three ways: cabled (instrument is powered), telemeter (depending power and other functions, sends data periodically throughout the day), and recovered (where instruments are telemetered, the data can also be stored onboard or on cards that can get recovered).

  • MIO’s do minimal processing and then the data is pushed to the Data Center (PowerScale).

  • Then a specific code, the Ingest Engine, notes the files coming in. Two nuances: cable data bypasses the ingest engine and goes directly to a queue where that queue is being monitored, and the Ingest Engine is looking at raw data files, and when it sees one comes in it will take its code, its logic for that specific instrument, process it and put it in that same queue. All of that code is homegrown and written in Python2 (upgrading to Python3 in the near future).

    • The queue is called Cupid (a third party software). It is a durable queue (fault tolerant) in the event that power is lost or the system crashes, it monitors itself and comes back up knowing where it left off.

    • On this queue there is AWIPS II (a software package monitoring the queue). EDEX is a Java product, also a part of AWIPS. AWIPS was developed by NOAA, and OOI doesn’t use the front end of the coding.

    • Data comes into Cupid, with the job to update the OOI database.

  • Postgres, the data works as a part of Cassandra and is a multithreaded base (very fault tolerant).

    • Raw data go into Cassandra.

      • Cassandra is a multi-node, multi-threaded database and also very fault tolerant. When data comes in, it gets committed at what we call a replication factor of three. It writes the data to 3 separate disks, so if 2 disks fail, you will always have your data. This also makes it highly performant so that when you pull data out, it can parallelize its queries.

      • There are also many layers to this on the back end, and [the data center?] can’t be considered a flow through system.

      • EDEX pulls the incoming data off the queue in manageable packets and writes it to Cassandra, and the goal of writing to Cassandra is to be as raw data-like as possible. They translate the data but do not change the data going into Cassandra.

      • Postgres keeps track of the metadata so that it knows how to access Cassandra. It keeps metadata about OOI instruments. It keeps tracking information about data going in and data going out, but it doesn’t store any of the observations. If we were to lose Postgres, Cassandra is useless. If we were to lose Cassandra, Postgres doesn’t have a purpose. So they work hand in hand.

    • Data is coming in quickly and there is a lot of it. Not a lot of logic is used, it’s mostly breaking down files. If the system is offline, data comes into the Cupid queue. Once the system is back online, it takes about 10 minutes to catch back up.

How does someone access OOI data? (these are also described in the OOI Data Bus)

  • Raw Data Server, provides raw instrument and engineering data via an Apache web server.

  • THREDDS, provides pre-computed scientific numerical data products with calibrations applied, alongside engineering data. Full resolution data sets are accessible by deployment and stream.

  • Data Explorer is the primary gateway to visualize and access OOI data. Data sets can be compared across regions and disciplines, and custom data views can be generated. Full datasets can be downloaded using ERDDAP.

    • ERDAPP, the underlying data server for Data Explorer, provides access to ~600 datasets, organized by OOI array. Data sets can be downloaded in common file formats; graphs and maps can also be generated.

  • M2M (machine-to-machine; main API) provides access to science and engineering data using both synchronous and asynchronous interfaces. NetCDF and JSON files are standard outputs. Users can access OOI data via the M2M API using MATLAB, Python, or R; some knowledge of programming is needed. There are tutorials available here.

  • JupyterHub, provides access to full resolution data sets and the raw data server, allowing users to share notebooks and process data in a large server environment. With dedicated (virtual) resources in JupyterHub and the enhanced computational power associated with graphics processing unites (GPUs), users can significantly speed up processing activities as they are able to directly access OOI data, no download required.

Is raw data direct from the sensor always available**?**
Mostly yes? In some cases, where a sensor internally applies something or there is a manufacturer-provided calibration.

  • AWIPS pulls the data out. This is an EDEX process using Postgres and Cassandra to bring the data back.

    • Calling EDEX is a process called Stream Engine (Stream Engine is written and maintained by the OOI CI team, in Python). Stream Engine receives a request, it calls EDEX, which brings the data back. It does some managing of QA/QC at that time.

      • OOI cannot change the data as it comes off the instrument, so it is changed as it comes out. This also applies to any calibrations. There are IONs (Integrated Ocean Network), like a brain function. Stream Engine tells ION what to do with the data, how the calibration is applied, and also is it a calculated field. Raw data is stored in Cassandra. There are no field calculations so if it’s a derived variable, that’s when that happens.

      • Stream Engine puts all of this together in a netCDF (no bigger than 300MB). When you pull data into a folder there are thousands of files, because the CI team is trying to be considerate to the network. Since Stream Engine was written, networks have gotten a lot better, they may make those files a little bit bigger in the future.

NSF OOI Users (who are they and how/why are they using OOI infrastructure/data)?#

  • Scientific & Research Community

    • Oceanographers & Marine Scientists – Use OOI data to study ocean circulation, climate change, ecosystem dynamics, and hypoxia.

    • Interdisciplinary Researchers – Includes meteorologists, data scientists, and engineers leveraging OOI technology and datasets.

    • Graduate & Postdoctoral Researchers – Utilize OOI datasets for dissertations, thesis work, and academic publications.

    • Modelers - Would like to (and some may actually) ingest OOI data for input or assimilation into models

  • Academic & Educational Institutions

    • University Professors & Educators – Incorporate OOI data into curriculum and research projects in oceanography, environmental science, and data analytics.

    • K-12 STEM Teachers – Use real-time OOI data and visualization tools to engage students in ocean science and climate education.

    • Students (K-12, Undergraduate, Graduate) – Benefit from OOI data for coursework, independent research, and citizen science projects.

  • Government Agencies & Policymakers

    • NSF – Primary funder and oversight body interested in the scientific advancements and outcomes of OOI research.

    • NOAA – Uses OOI data for climate modeling, hurricane forecasting, and ocean monitoring.

    • NASA – Integrates OOI oceanographic data with satellite observations for Earth science applications.

    • EPA – Utilize OOI data for policy decisions on marine conservation, pollution control, and ecosystem management.

    • Local & State Governments – Use OOI findings to inform coastal planning, fisheries management, and climate adaptation policies.

NSF OOI Glossary and Acronym List#

Please follow the links above to get to the full OOI glossary and acronym page. Below are a list of key terms that are frequently used during OOIFB and DSC meetings.

  • Alfresco: A document management system used to store all OOI documentation.

  • API: Application Programming Interface. Allows two distinct applications to interact and share data seamlessly with each other.

  • AWIPS: Advanced Weather Interactive Programming System. A core meteorological computing and display system that ingests radar, satellite, and observational data to help forecasters visualize conditions, predict the weather, and issue timely watches and warnings to the public.

  • CASSANDRA- database storing instrument observations. Needs PostgreSQL to access data.

  • CGSN- Coastal and Global Scale Nodes of the OOI (Coastal Pioneer, Global Irminger, and Station Papa arrays).

  • CI- Cyberinfrastructure (OOI CI Team- at WHOI and OSU).

    • The implementing organizations responsible for OOI software, computing hardware, and data lifecycle and data quality management.

  • Data Explorer- Newest user interface implemented. It was implemented to replace OOINET (legacy user interface) with the intention of providing the user an easy to use interface to explore and download OOI data. Data is pre-processed and staged every 24 hours (overnight ET).

  • DSC- Data Systems Committee; a subcommittee of the OOIFB that is largely focused on reviewing/discussing data services policies and practices of the OOI Facility that will lead to more efficient and effective scientific use of OOI data.

  • EDEX- Environmental Data Exchange software (middleware) that is maintained by CI developers. It controls how data are written to and retrieved from the Cassandra and PostgreSQL databases. It is written in Java.

  • ERDDAP Server- The OOI ERDDAP (NOAA’s Environmental Research Division’s Data Access Program) Server provides a simple, consistent way to access and download OOI data. Datasets can be downloaded in common file formats (e.g., CSV, NetCDF, or JSON), with the capability of creating graphs and maps, and used in several different applications and utilities such as Python, R, Javascript, and MATLAB.

    • ERDDAP User Guide: https://erddap.dataexplorer.oceanobservatories.org/erddap/index.html

  • FAIR (Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, and Reuse - Guiding principles for scientific data management and stewardship. https://www.go-fair.org/fair-principles/

  • FDCHP (Flux Direct Covariance High-Power) - these are commonly known in the community as Direct Covariance Flux Systems (DCFS) for direct measurement of momentum and buoyancy flux, distinguished from indirect or “bluk” estimates: https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0426(1998)015<0547:DCFEFM>2.0.CO;2)

  • ION function - Python code executed at time of data set request (called by StreamEngine). ION functions (oceanobservatories/ion-functions) execute logic to apply variables (e.g.calibration) as well as create calculated data elements.

  • PowerScale- a scale out network-attached storage platform for high-volume storage, backup and archiving of unstructured data.

  • JupyterHub- OOIs virtual public cloud environment allows users to utilize OOI processing resources to code logic to analyze both processed and raw data in place without the need to download data. The environment has a large number of processors and memory, giving all users access to a robust computing environment regardless of funding.

    • https://jupyter.oceanobservatories.org/

  • M2M- machine to machine method of data access (API) via both synchronous and I asynchronous modes. Data is calculated at time of request.

  • METBK (METeorology, BulK) - a collection of instrumentation for measurement of meteorological state variables (air temp, humidity, wind speed, etc) that can be used to estimate air-sea fluxes via bulk formulas. Distinguished from direct covariance flux estimates

  • MIO- Marine Implementing Organizations for the OOI (University of Washington [UW], Oregon State University [OSU], and Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution [WHOI]).

    • The organizations that assemble, maintain, deploy, and recover the marine instrumentation and infrastructure that compose the at-sea portions of OOI.

    • RCA - Regional Cabled Array - University of Washington.

    • EA - Endurance Array - Oregon State University.

    • CGSN - Coastal and Global Scale Nodes (Coastal Pioneer, Global Irminger, and Station Papa arrays) - Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution.

  • NETCDF- Common file structure that OOI delivers data in.

  • NSF- United States National Science Foundation.

  • ONC- Ocean Networks Canada**. An in**itiative of the University of Victoria (UVic), a Major Research Facility of Canada, and a Decade Implementing Partner for the United Nations Decade of Ocean Science for Sustainable Development (2021–2030). ONC operates world-leading deep-sea and coastal observatories on Canada’s Pacific, Atlantic, and Arctic coasts, and in the Southern (Antarctic) Ocean. Its cabled infrastructure provides continuous power and internet connectivity to scientific instruments, cameras, and more than 12,000 ocean sensors. ONC also manages mobile and land-based assets, including coastal radar.

  • OOI- Ocean Observatories Initiative.

  • OOIFB- Ocean Observatories Initiative Facilities Board.

  • OOINET - Legacy user interface for plotting and downloading data. All data is calculated at time of request. Data plotted is heavily decimated - 999 data points are used.

  • PI Data - this term is variably used to mean data from PI Instruments (which is shared via a RCA website) or post-processed data provided by PI’s rather than by OOI.

  • PI Instruments - instruments funded separately from OOI and provided by an PI for OOI to deploy (see longer discussion above)

  • PostgreSQL- database storing metadata about instrument data and data delivery. Works with Cassandra to provide OOI database functions.

  • Redmine- The OOI instance of the Redmine project management web application (https://redmine.oceanobservatories.org) is used to track work tasks and as an informational archive of work completed (individuals will refer to these as “redmine tickets”).

  • PMO- Program Management Office for the OOI (located at WHOI).

  • QA/QC- Quality Assurance/Quality Control.

    • QARTOD (Quality Assurance/Quality Control for Real-Time Oceanographic Data). OOI has committed to implementing available QARTOD tests where appropriate. QARTOD is well-documented and actively maintained with an engaged user-base across multiple data collection and repository programs. It has a publicly available code-base with standardized tests and flag definitions that result in simplified, easy-to-interpret results. However, some instruments deployed by OOI, such as seismic sensors and multispectral sensors, are not well-suited towards QARTOD. For instruments for which there is no existing QARTOD manual, such as seawater pCO2, OOI is implementing “QARTOD-like” quality control (QC).

    • For more information: https://oceanobservatories.org/quality-control/

  • SOC- Science Oversight Committee. This committee provides program oversight on matters impacting NSF OOI science requirements, data delivery, and science user engagement. The OOI lead PI chairs the committee, and committee members include the PIs from the OOI Marine Implementing Organizations. The U.S. National Science Foundation, Division of Ocean Sciences Program Officer(s) and the Chair and Administrator of the Ocean Observatories Initiative Facility Board serve as ex officio members.

  • Stream Engine- the back-end query engine for the OOI CyberInfrastructure. It exposes a JSON-based HTTP interface which allows for the querying of full resolution or subsampled data from the data store and execution of numerous data product algorithms on this data. The results are returned to the user in a JSON data structure for synchronous results or asynchronously as NetCDF4, CSV or TSV.

  • THREDDS- Thematic Real-time Environmental Distributed Data Services data server is maintained by Unidata. The goal of it is to provide students, educators and researchers with coherent access to a large collection of real-time and archived datasets from a variety of environmental data sources at a number of distributed server sites. The OOI THREDDS Data Server provides a catalog of pre-generated datasets for some of the most commonly requested instruments that the OOI maintains. You can access this catalog using your favorite OPeNDAP toolboxes, like Pydap for Python or the NetCDF functions in Matlab.

    • https://thredds.dataexplorer.oceanobservatories.org/thredds/catalog/ooigoldcopy/public/catalog.html
